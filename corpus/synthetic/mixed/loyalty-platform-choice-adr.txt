SYNTHETIC - illustrative, not from any real company. Written for a portfolio RAG project; does not describe any real architecture decision.

Architecture Decision Record - Loyalty Platform. ADR ID ADR-002. Status Accepted. Date 2025-09-18.

Context: the loyalty program (LOY-POL-001, LOY-POL-002) needs a points ledger that supports accrual, redemption, tier calculation, and expiry, all with strong consistency guarantees - a customer's displayed balance has to match what is actually redeemable at checkout, with no eventual-consistency window where a customer sees points that a concurrent transaction has already spent. Three options were evaluated: build the ledger in-house on top of the existing transactional database, adopt a commercial loyalty-platform-as-a-service product, or extend the order management system's own event log (see ADR-001, the OMS choice record) to also carry loyalty state rather than standing up a separate system.

Decision: build the points ledger in-house, on the existing transactional database, rather than adopting a commercial platform or extending the OMS event log.

Rationale, in three parts. First, against the commercial platform option: every commercial loyalty-platform-as-a-service product evaluated used eventual consistency for balance updates by default, trading a small delay for horizontal scale, which is the right tradeoff for most of their customers but directly conflicts with the strong-consistency requirement stated in the context above - a customer should never see a redeemable balance that a concurrent transaction has already spent, and no evaluated vendor offered a strong-consistency mode without a custom enterprise contract at a cost disproportionate to this program's transaction volume. Second, against extending the OMS event log: this was rejected specifically because of the reasoning already captured in ADR-001 - keeping loyalty state as a single source of truth queried by the OMS, rather than replicated into it, was the explicit design choice made there to avoid the class of consistency bug that caused the loyalty double-credit incident, POS-ERR-3210, and extending the OMS event log to carry loyalty state directly would have reintroduced exactly that risk rather than avoiding it. Third, in favor of building in-house on the existing transactional database: the existing database already provides the strong-consistency guarantees needed, the team already operates it in production, and the actual ledger logic - accrual, redemption, tier threshold checks, expiry - is not large or complex enough on its own to justify either a new vendor relationship or a new piece of infrastructure to operate.

Consequences: the team owns the full correctness burden for the ledger, including the exact class of bug that caused POS-ERR-3210 - building in-house does not prevent that failure mode, it only means the team is fully responsible for preventing and fixing it rather than depending on a vendor's own reliability engineering. The corrective actions in the POS-ERR-3210 postmortem, specifically the ledger-level uniqueness constraint added as a second line of defense independent of application-level idempotency logic, are a direct consequence of this decision - a commercial platform's internal correctness guarantees would not have been the team's problem to solve, whereas building in-house made it exactly that. This tradeoff was accepted deliberately, not overlooked - the alternative options both introduced worse problems of their own, as detailed in the rationale above, and the team judged owning this specific correctness burden preferable to either eventual consistency or a repeat of the OMS coupling problem.

A note on hindsight, added after POS-ERR-3210: this decision is not being revisited as a result of that incident. The incident was caused by an idempotency-key bug in the accrual retry logic, which is an application-layer bug independent of the underlying database choice - the same bug class could equally have occurred against a commercial platform's write API if that platform's own retry/idempotency contract were misunderstood or misused by the integrating team. The corrective actions taken (idempotency key fix, ledger-level uniqueness constraint) directly address the actual root cause without requiring a platform migration, which would have been a large, risky undertaking to address a bug that was not actually about the platform choice at all.
